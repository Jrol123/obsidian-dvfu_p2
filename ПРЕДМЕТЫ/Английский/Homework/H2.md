## № 14 (p. 28)
**1. What is the aim of this research?** The aim of the research is to identify and analyse the life goals of the Russian population and to determine what these goals reveal about the true ideological structure of society and the social trajectory of the country's development. In particular, the researchers aimed to find out whether Russians engage in long-term goal-setting and how their goals are distributed between utilitarian and idealistic motives.

**2. What problem is studied in the paper?** The paper studies the problem of **detachment from long-term goal-setting** in Russian society: almost half of Russians live from day to day without clear life goals, merely reproducing their existing way of life. The authors also examine the predominance of utilitarian goals (reflecting unresolved social and domestic problems) and the danger that a significant portion of society is excluded from the goal-setting process, which underuses the society's "resource of modernization" and hinders social progress.

**3. Which data/evidence is used for the purposes of this research?** The research is based on **empirical survey data collected in 2017**: respondents' answers about their life goals, desired time frames for achieving them, and the nature of their goals (material, non-monetary, or non-measurable). The data are presented statistically (percentages and averages: 52.4% have a specific goal, 25.9% cannot name any goal, 21.7% believe they have achieved everything, an average time frame of about 5 years, etc.). The authors also rely on **previous theoretical and empirical studies** showing that social well-being correlates with the goals society sets for itself.

**4. Is the sample representative for the study?** Yes, according to the text the sample is **representative**: it comprised **700 people living in different regions of Russia**, with a balanced socio-demographic profile — 53% women and 47% men, an average age of about 46, 34% holding advanced educational degrees, 48% living in large cities and regional capitals, and an average per capita income of 19,000 roubles a month. This coverage of regions, genders, ages, education levels and incomes allows the results to be generalised to the population. (Critically, one may note that 700 respondents is a relatively modest size for a country as large as Russia, but the authors describe the sample as representative.)

**5. What methodology is used?** The methodology is a **sociological survey (poll) based on a representative sample**, combined with **statistical analysis** of the responses (percentages, average ages and time frames) and **classification (typologisation)** of the reported goals. Participants were asked whether their goal implied a material result, a non-monetary result, or something not measurable monetarily at all; on this basis the goals were categorised as 'purely utilitarian', 'mixed', and 'purely idealistic'. The interpretation of the results is supported by earlier theoretical and empirical studies.

**6. What are the findings of the researchers?**

- 48% of Russians live from day to day without planning for the future; only 52.4% have a specific life goal.
- 25.9% cannot name any goal beyond everyday routines (work, housework, family); 21.7% (average age 67) believe they have already achieved everything they can.
- The average desired time frame for achieving goals is about 5 years; the longest (8–9 years) concern loans and children's future, the shortest (about 3 years) concern travel and purchases.
- Three goal categories were identified: **purely utilitarian** (buying a home/car, renovation, promotion), **purely idealistic** (starting a family, having children; professional/creative goals such as defending a thesis or learning an instrument — largely characteristic of women without children), and **mixed** (the most popular category, combining creative, pragmatic and professional-self-realisation motives).
- The more educated and higher paid a person is, the more likely he or she is to have a goal.
- Overall, Russia shows "a high degree of detachment from long-term goal-setting"; a dangerous new balance between utilitarian and idealistic interests has taken hold, while a significant part of society is completely excluded from goal-setting.

**7. How can the results of the study be used in practice?** The results can be used to **predetermine (forecast) the social trajectory of the country's development** and to design social policy aimed at reversing the dangerous trend. They help identify the underused "resource of modernization" (people in their prime years without socially meaningful goals), show which social and domestic problems push people towards purely utilitarian goals, and can inform state programmes, educational and cultural policies intended to stimulate long-term goal-setting and the creation of values of global interest. The data can also serve as a basis for further sociological research.

**8. What are the limitations of this research?**

- The **sample size is relatively small** (700 respondents) for a country as large as Russia.
- The data are **self-reported**: goals were classified according to the respondents' own views, which may be subjective or ambiguous (the boundary between utilitarian and idealistic motives is not always clear).
- The study is a **one-time (cross-sectional) snapshot of 2017**, so it does not show changes in goal-setting over time.
- The abstract does not report the **margin of error, the questionnaire design, or statistical validation** of the categories.
- Some conclusions (e.g., about the "resource of modernization" or the "dangerous turn") are **interpretations** rather than directly measured facts.
